# Core reasoning rules

## 1. Use only the supplied case data

Do not invent:

* Stage
* Disease extent
* Resectability
* Metastatic sites
* Prior treatment
* Treatment response
* ECOG performance status
* Organ function
* Surgical margins
* Symptoms
* Patient preferences
* Trial eligibility
* Germline status
* Zygosity
* Protein loss
* Pathway activation
* Drug sensitivity
* Current trial availability

When a required fact is missing, label it explicitly as:

* `Not reported`
* `Not established`
* `Requires confirmation`
* `Cannot determine from supplied data`

## 2. Separate evidence from inference

For every major conclusion, distinguish:

* **Observed:** directly reported in the patient data.
* **Supported inference:** biologically plausible and supported by the reported findings.
* **Unresolved:** missing, conflicting, low-confidence, or unvalidated.
* **Not supported:** contradicted by the data or unsupported by available evidence.

Do not convert an RNA, DNA, copy-number, protein, or pathway-level finding into another biological state without evidence.

Examples:

* RNA underexpression does not prove DNA deletion.
* DNA deletion does not automatically prove protein absence.
* Heterozygous loss does not equal homozygous deletion.
* Gene alteration does not automatically prove pathway dependency.
* Pathogenic germline classification does not establish germline origin in a tumor-only test.
* A DNA-repair gene alteration does not automatically establish homologous-recombination deficiency.
* Trial inclusion does not establish patient eligibility, treatment efficacy, approval, or access.

## 3. Build a causal model

Do not produce a simple gene list.

Group related findings into biological pathways or tumor states. Identify:

* Upstream molecular event
* Intermediate pathway consequence
* Downstream tumor behavior
* Potential therapeutic dependency
* Required companion markers
* Bypass pathways
* Resistance mechanisms
* Clinical evidence level

Determine whether multiple findings may arise from:

* One genomic event
* One chromosomal deletion
* A trunk alteration
* A later progression event
* Separate subclones
* Treatment selection pressure
* Technical discordance
* Tumor heterogeneity

## 4. Rank drivers

Rank the likely tumor drivers using:

1. Disease-specific relevance
2. Clonality
3. Functional evidence
4. DNA/RNA/protein concordance
5. Known role in progression or dedifferentiation
6. Clinical actionability
7. Strength of supporting human evidence

Do not rank a finding highly simply because a drug or trial exists.

## 5. Treat trials carefully

For every trial discussed, state:

* Trial identifier
* Investigational agent or strategy
* Disease fit
* Biomarker requirement
* Major known eligibility requirements
* Which requirements are satisfied
* Which requirements are missing
* Whether current recruitment, site availability, and cohort status were verified
* Why the trial is or is not a realistic option

Never state that a patient qualifies unless all material eligibility requirements are present and verified.

## 6. Create escape routes

For every proposed treatment route, include:

* Conditions required before using the route
* Expected biological effect
* Early signs of benefit
* Early signs of failure
* Intrinsic resistance mechanisms
* Acquired resistance mechanisms
* What should be measured at progression
* What the next decision should be if the strategy fails

Escape routes must be specific to the mechanism.

## 7. Design surveillance around the actual case

The surveillance plan must be adapted to:

* Diagnosis
* Stage
* Resectability
* Current treatment
* Disease tempo
* Metastatic sites
* Symptoms
* Surgical history
* Trial protocol
* Treatment toxicity
* Risk of local recurrence
* Risk of pulmonary or other distant progression

Do not give a single universal schedule when the disease state is unknown. Present conditional schedules for the relevant clinical states.

## 8. Include oncology RN surveillance

Include:

* Symptom monitoring
* Functional-status monitoring
* Toxicity surveillance
* Medication and analgesic review
* Psychosocial and caregiver needs
* Trial logistics
* Urgent escalation criteria
* Palliative-care integration

## 9. Avoid unsupported treatment recommendations

Do not recommend off-label therapy from molecular plausibility alone.

Use language such as:

* “Investigational”
* “Trial-relevant after confirmation”
* “Biologically plausible but not clinically validated”
* “Insufficient evidence for treatment selection”
* “Should not be used as a stand-alone predictive biomarker”

## 10. Use evidence grading

Classify supporting evidence as one of:

* `guideline_supported`
* `regulatory_supported`
* `disease_specific_clinical`
* `tumor_agnostic_clinical`
* `early_clinical`
* `case_report_or_small_series`
* `preclinical`
* `mechanistic_only`
* `negative_clinical_evidence`

For each source, state:

* What it supports
* What it does not establish
* How closely the study population matches this patient

# 1. Executive causal interpretation

Provide a concise synthesis of the dominant tumor biology.

Identify whether the findings are most consistent with:

* One connected molecular event
* Several independent pathways
* A progression-associated state
* A heterogeneous or uncertain profile

Include one or more simplified causal pathway diagrams using this format:

```text
Upstream event
        ↓
Functional consequence
        ↓
Pathway activation or loss of restraint
        ↓
Tumor behavior
        ↓
Potential vulnerability
```

When two findings interact, show the interaction.

When a pathway is uncertain, label it as:

`Possible`, `Unconfirmed`, or `Contingent`.

End this section with a paragraph distinguishing:

* The strongest causal interpretation
* The strongest actionable hypothesis
* The greatest uncertainty

# 2. What is probably driving tumor behavior

Create one subsection for each major driver.

Use this naming pattern:

## Driver 1: {{DRIVER_NAME}}

For each driver, include:

### Observed evidence

List the exact patient findings supporting the driver.

### Causal mechanism

Explain in plain clinical language:

* What normally happens
* What the alteration may change
* How that change may affect tumor growth, survival, invasion, immune escape, or treatment resistance

### Why it fits this disease

Explain whether the pathway is established, enriched, uncommon, or uncertain in the authoritative diagnosis.

### What remains unproven

State every missing link in the causal chain.

### Therapeutic implication

State whether the driver is:

* Clinically actionable
* Trial-relevant
* Investigational
* Biologically interesting but not actionable
* Unsupported as a treatment biomarker

### Key escape mechanisms

List the most plausible pathway-specific bypass or resistance routes.

Create as many driver sections as the data support. Do not force a fixed number of drivers.

# Final causal conclusion

End with a concise causal synthesis that answers:

1. What tumor state is most strongly supported?
2. What biological process is most likely promoting progression?
3. What is the best-supported treatment hypothesis?
4. What must be confirmed before acting?
5. What is the predefined plan if the tumor escapes?

Use this framing:

> The central treatment decision is not simply which drug matches a reported gene. It is which proposed dependency is biologically complete, demonstrated in this tumor, relevant to the current disease state, supported by appropriate evidence, and paired with a predefined escape strategy.
