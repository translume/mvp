# 1. Executive causal interpretation

The findings are most consistent with a **possible connected 9p21 event** affecting neighboring MTAP and CDKN2-family genes, accompanied by a separate and less certain DNA-damage checkpoint profile. The 9p21 pattern is disease-relevant in dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma, but its deletion boundaries, zygosity, subclonality, protein consequences, and functional dependencies are unresolved.

```text
Possible 9p21 genomic loss
        ↓
MTAP copy loss + RNA underexpression
        +
CDKN2A RNA underexpression
        +
possible but discordant CDKN2B abnormality
        ↓
Two possible consequences:
  1. Contingent MTAP-null/MTA–PRMT5 metabolic state
  2. Possible loss of p16/p15-mediated cell-cycle restraint
        ↓
Possible PRMT5 dependence + increased CDK4/6–RB signaling
        ↓
Metabolic vulnerability and/or accelerated G1-to-S progression
        ↓
Investigational MTAP-directed or CDK4/6-directed trial hypotheses
```

Both branches remain incomplete:

```text
MTAP copy loss and low RNA
        ↓
Possible complete MTAP loss
        ↓
Possible MTA accumulation and residual PRMT5 dependence
        ↓
MTA-cooperative PRMT5-inhibitor hypothesis

Required: homozygous deletion + malignant-cell MTAP protein loss
Unconfirmed: tumor MTA accumulation and PRMT5 dependence
```

```text
CDKN2A RNA underexpression ± possible CDKN2B loss
        ↓
Possible p16/p15 protein loss
        ↓
Possible release of CDK4/6 activity
        ↓
RB phosphorylation → E2F-driven proliferation
        ↓
CDK4/6-inhibitor trial hypothesis

Required: qualifying DNA alteration + retained functional RB
Unconfirmed: p16/p15 loss, RB retention, and CDK4/6 dependence
```

A separate checkpoint hypothesis is substantially weaker:

```text
CHEK2 c.846+4_846+7del
        +
low-confidence unspecified TP53 splice-region call
        ↓
Possible combined impairment of damage-induced arrest/apoptosis
        ↓
Possible progression-associated checkpoint-deficient state
        ↓
No validated therapeutic dependency

Unconfirmed: CHEK2 origin/biallelic loss, exact TP53 event, and HRD
```

- **Strongest causal interpretation:** A possible broader 9p21 lesion is the most coherent unifying event. If it produces p16/p15 loss while RB remains functional, altered cell-cycle control is the most plausible direct promoter of proliferation. MTAP loss may be a co-deletion that creates a vulnerability rather than the primary growth driver.
- **Strongest actionable hypothesis:** A disease-matched CDK-pathway trial is the most directly connected to dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma, but it requires a qualifying CDKN2A/B alteration, retained RB, advanced/unresectable disease, and other clinical criteria. MTAP-directed trials become relevant only after complete MTAP loss is established.
- **Greatest uncertainty:** Whether the reported RNA and copy-number findings represent complete, clonal, protein-level loss and true pathway dependency. CHEK2/TP53 and homologous-recombination biology are even less resolved.

# 2. What is probably driving tumor behavior

## Driver 1: Possible CDKN2A/CDKN2B–RB cell-cycle checkpoint dysregulation

### Observed evidence

- CDKN2A RNA underexpression is reported.
- CDKN2B findings are discordant and low confidence; the direction of RNA expression cannot be assigned.
- MTAP copy-number loss raises the possibility of a neighboring 9p21 event.
- No CDKN2A/B absolute copy number, zygosity, deletion boundaries, or protein findings are reported.
- RB1 sequence, copy number, protein retention, phosphorylation, and function are not reported.

### Causal mechanism

p16^INK4A and p15^INK4B normally inhibit CDK4/6. Their functional loss can permit CDK4/6-mediated RB phosphorylation, release E2F transcription factors, and accelerate G1-to-S progression. This could promote proliferation and dedifferentiated tumor behavior.

The chain remains conditional: reduced CDKN2A RNA does not prove p16 protein loss, and an upstream CDK4/6 inhibitor generally requires RB to remain present and functional.

### Why it fits this disease

CDKN2A/B alterations and cell-cycle programs are enriched in dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. Disease-specific series support this pathway as biologically relevant, but prevalence does not establish that this individual tumor is CDK4/6-dependent.

### What remains unproven

- CDKN2A or CDKN2B DNA deletion.
- Homozygous versus heterozygous or subclonal loss.
- p16 or p15 protein loss in malignant cells.
- Retained RB1 sequence, copy number, protein, and function.
- CDK4/6-mediated RB engagement.
- Absence of cyclin E–CDK2 or other bypass signaling.
- Clinical sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibition.

### Therapeutic implication

**Trial-relevant and investigational after confirmation.** CDKN2A RNA underexpression alone is not a sufficient treatment biomarker and does not support off-label CDK4/6 inhibition.

NCT04040205 includes a dedicated osteosarcoma/dedifferentiated-chondrosarcoma cohort using abemaciclib in CDK-pathway-altered sarcoma. The documents report requirements including a qualifying pathway alteration, retained RB protein, advanced/metastatic or locally advanced unresectable disease, prior anthracycline treatment unless contraindicated, and adequate clinical/laboratory status.

### Key escape mechanisms

- Pre-existing or acquired RB1 loss or functional inactivation.
- Cyclin E–CDK2 activation.
- Alternative cyclin/CDK activation.
- Independently active PI3K–AKT or other survival signaling; AKT2 RNA overexpression alone does not establish this.
- Subclonal 9p21 loss, leaving resistant p16/p15-intact populations.
- A cytostatic response without durable tumor control.

## Driver 2: Possible MTAP-null methionine-salvage/PRMT5 dependency

### Observed evidence

- MTAP DNA copy-number loss is reported.
- MTAP RNA underexpression is reported.
- Tumor content was 80%.
- Homozygous deletion, deletion focality, malignant-cell protein loss, tumor MTA accumulation, and PRMT5-output changes are not reported.

### Causal mechanism

MTAP normally metabolizes methylthioadenosine in methionine and adenine salvage. Complete MTAP loss can permit MTA accumulation, partially constrain PRMT5, and increase dependence on residual PRMT5 activity. This provides the rationale for MTA-cooperative PRMT5 inhibitors and, mechanistically, MAT2A-directed approaches.

This tumor has evidence of reduced MTAP dosage and expression, but it has not been shown to be MTAP-null or to possess the proposed metabolic state.

### Why it fits this disease

A disease-specific genomic series reported MTAP alteration in approximately 19% of dedifferentiated chondrosarcomas. This supports a genuine disease subgroup but not patient-specific drug sensitivity.

### What remains unproven

- Homozygous rather than heterozygous MTAP deletion.
- Clonality and cancer-cell fraction of the deletion.
- Absent MTAP protein in malignant cells.
- MTA accumulation in the tumor microenvironment.
- PRMT5 dependence or target engagement.
- Whether MTAP-positive stroma or tumor subclones neutralize the phenotype.
- Clinical benefit from PRMT5 or MAT2A inhibition in dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma.

### Therapeutic implication

**Investigational and biomarker-contingent.** MTAP RNA underexpression and unspecified copy loss should not be used as stand-alone predictive biomarkers.

Two supplied trial examples are:

- **NCT05245500:** MRTX1719/BMS-986504 in advanced solid tumors with homozygous MTAP deletion.
- **NCT06914128:** BAY 3713372 in solid tumors requiring homozygous MTAP deletion; the supplied memo also reports measurable disease by RECIST 1.1 and ECOG 0–1 requirements.

Neither registry establishes dedifferentiated-chondrosarcoma efficacy or this patient’s eligibility.

### Key escape mechanisms

- A retained functional MTAP allele.
- MTAP-positive tumor subclones.
- MTAP-positive stromal or endothelial cells clearing extracellular MTA.
- Failure to accumulate MTA despite genomic deletion.
- Inadequate drug exposure or PRMT5 target engagement.
- Restoration or bypass of PRMT5-dependent RNA-processing and methylation functions.
- Growth driven independently by the neighboring CDKN2–RB pathway.

## Driver 3: Unconfirmed CHEK2–TP53 checkpoint impairment

### Observed evidence

- CHEK2 c.846+4_846+7del was reported as loss of function.
- ClinVar evidence supplied in the documents supports abnormal splicing and pathogenic/likely pathogenic classification in the germline predisposition setting.
- No matched normal was analyzed.
- CHEK2 tumor variant allele fraction, local copy number, loss of heterozygosity, and second-hit status are not reported.
- A low-confidence TP53 splice-region call was reported without an exact variant.
- No homologous-recombination genomic or functional phenotype is reported.

### Causal mechanism

CHEK2 participates in DNA-damage-induced cell-cycle arrest, repair signaling, and apoptosis through effectors including p53. A damaging splice alteration could weaken this checkpoint. Independently validated TP53 dysfunction could further reduce arrest or apoptosis and may contribute to progression or dedifferentiation.

Possible dual-checkpoint impairment is distinct from homologous-recombination deficiency and cannot be used to infer PARP sensitivity.

### Why it fits this disease

TP53 alterations are disease-relevant and may occur as later progression events in dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. CHEK2’s role in this histology is not established. Disease prevalence cannot validate the current low-confidence TP53 call.

### What remains unproven

- Germline versus somatic CHEK2 origin.
- Monoallelic versus biallelic tumor inactivation.
- Expression of an abnormal CHEK2 transcript in tumor cells.
- CHEK2 protein loss or functional checkpoint failure.
- The exact TP53 variant and its validity.
- Combined CHEK2–TP53 dysfunction.
- Homologous-recombination deficiency.
- Sensitivity to PARP, ATR, WEE1, or other DNA-damage-response therapy.

### Therapeutic implication

**Not validated as a treatment biomarker.** CHEK2’s immediate relevance is confirmation and possible hereditary-cancer evaluation. The supplied tumor-agnostic phase II evidence reported no responses among 14 CHEK2-altered tumors treated with olaparib. CHEK2 alone should not select a PARP inhibitor.

### Key escape mechanisms

- Retention of a functional CHEK2 allele.
- Residual normal CHEK2 transcript or protein.
- Compensation by other checkpoint kinases or p53-independent arrest.
- Alternative DNA-repair pathways.
- Absence of a true functional TP53 alteration.
- Lack of an actual homologous-recombination defect.

# 5. Treatment framework and escape routes

## Route 1: Local-control–dominant management

### Appropriate when

Disease is localized, locally recurrent but technically controllable, or oligometastatic with all clinically important sites amenable to local treatment. Resectability and current extent are not reported.

### Core strategy

A sarcoma multidisciplinary team should determine the role of surgery, radiation, ablation, or other metastasis-directed treatment. The chest-wall specimen location alone is insufficient to select a local modality.

### Evidence level

**Cannot determine from supplied data.** The two documents do not provide a local-therapy evidence source or establish the patient’s local disease setting.

### Required confirmation

Current staging, anatomical imaging, prior operative and radiation records, margin status, reconstruction considerations, recurrence history, and patient fitness.

### Expected benefit signal

Complete control of known local disease, expected postoperative or post-radiation changes without enlarging viable tumor, symptom improvement, and absence of new distant disease.

### Early failure or resistance signals

Residual or enlarging local tumor, positive or indeterminate margins, rapid postoperative recurrence, new distant lesions, worsening local pain, wound compromise, or functional decline.

### Intrinsic escape routes

Unrecognized multifocal or distant disease, anatomically unresectable involvement, microscopic extension beyond the treatment field, or pre-existing heterogeneous resistant subclones.

### Acquired escape routes

Local recurrence after treatment or emergence of distant disease from occult micrometastases.

### Action at escape

Re-stage, confirm viable progression when imaging is ambiguous, biopsy a clinically meaningful progressing lesion when it could alter treatment, and reassess salvage local therapy versus systemic treatment.

## Route 2: Disease-appropriate nontrial systemic therapy

### Appropriate when

Disease is unresectable, metastatic, measurably progressive, symptomatic, or organ-threatening and a trial is unavailable, unsuitable, or not preferred.

### Core strategy

Selection should be based on the complete dedifferentiated-chondrosarcoma clinical setting and prior treatment history. The supplied documents do not identify or evidence-grade a standard regimen; therefore, no agent can be prescribed from this record.

### Evidence level

**Cannot determine from supplied documents.** No standard-systemic-therapy source or patient treatment history was provided.

### Required confirmation

Disease setting, need for immediate treatment, prior agents and responses, anthracycline exposure, performance status, organ function, comorbidities, and patient goals.

### Expected benefit signal

Radiographic shrinkage or durable stability using a consistent response method, symptom improvement, reduced analgesic requirement, and preserved or improved function.

### Early failure or resistance signals

New lesions, unequivocal growth, mixed response with a threatening progressing site, worsening symptoms, declining function, or toxicity without clinical benefit.

### Intrinsic escape routes

Baseline tumor heterogeneity, pathway independence from the selected regimen, resistant dedifferentiated subclones, or inability to deliver adequate treatment intensity.

### Acquired escape routes

Selection of resistant clones, pathway rewiring, target loss, or treatment-limiting cumulative toxicity. Mechanism-specific escape cannot be defined until a regimen is selected.

### Action at escape

Stop an ineffective or intolerable regimen, re-stage, consider biopsy/re-sequencing when results could guide a trial, assess local treatment for limited progression, and intensify supportive care.

# 7. Adaptive surveillance for treatment escape

## Isolated local progression

- Confirm with comparison imaging and dedicated local assessment; biopsy if treatment effect versus viable tumor is uncertain and the result would alter management.
- Reassess surgery, radiation, ablation, or another local modality through the sarcoma board.
- Continuing systemic treatment may be reasonable if all other disease remains controlled and the isolated site can be treated locally, but this depends on the regimen, protocol, and patient condition.
- If progression occurs during a targeted trial, reassess target retention and pathway escape when tissue is available.

## Single-organ oligoprogression

- Re-stage completely to confirm limited progression.
- Biopsy is useful when the progressing lesion may represent a resistant subclone or when the result would guide a new trial.
- Local treatment may preserve an otherwise effective systemic strategy if permitted by the protocol and clinically appropriate.
- Reassess MTAP status, RB1/RB protein, CDKN2A/B status, and relevant pathway markers according to the treatment received.

## Mixed response

Assume heterogeneity until evaluated.

Assess:

- Whether responding and progressing lesions share the 9p21 event.
- Clonality and subclonality of MTAP/CDKN2A/B loss.
- MTAP protein or RB target loss in the progressing lesion.
- Acquired RB1 loss or cyclin E–CDK2 bypass after CDK4/6-directed treatment.
- Retained MTAP expression or failed PRMT5 target engagement after MTAP-directed treatment.
- New resistance alterations or parallel pathway activation.
- Imaging artifact, necrosis, treatment effect, or differences in measurement technique.

## Generalized progression

- Stop an ineffective therapy when progression is confirmed and continued treatment lacks clinical benefit, subject to trial protocol and clinical judgment.
- Re-stage to define new disease burden and organ threat.
- Re-biopsy when the result could change treatment, particularly after targeted therapy or when the current specimen is old or discordant.
- Repeat molecular profiling when seeking a new trial or evaluating acquired resistance.
- Reassess local palliation for threatening or symptomatic sites.
- Intensify symptom control and palliative care alongside consideration of a new trial or disease-appropriate systemic strategy.

# Final causal conclusion

The tumor state most strongly supported is an **incompletely defined 9p21-altered state** linking MTAP dosage reduction with possible CDKN2-family checkpoint impairment. The biological process most likely to promote progression is loss of cell-cycle restraint through the CDKN2–CDK4/6–RB axis, but this remains contingent on p16/p15 loss and retained functional RB. MTAP loss may provide a parallel metabolic vulnerability, but complete MTAP-null status and the MTA–PRMT5 phenotype have not been demonstrated.

The best-supported treatment hypothesis is therefore not a specific drug but a **confirmation-driven trial sequence**: first determine whether there is a qualifying CDKN2A/B alteration with retained RB for the disease-matched CDK4/6 trial; separately determine whether homozygous MTAP loss supports an MTAP-selected trial. CHEK2 should prompt germline and allelic clarification, not PARP-inhibitor selection.

If the tumor escapes, the predefined plan is to classify progression as isolated, oligoprogressive, mixed, or generalized; use local therapy when limited progression can be controlled; biopsy and re-profile a progressing lesion when the result could reveal RB loss, MTAP target loss, or another resistance mechanism; stop ineffective therapy at generalized progression; and intensify supportive care.

> The central treatment decision is not simply which drug matches a reported gene. It is which proposed dependency is biologically complete, demonstrated in this tumor, relevant to the current disease state, supported by appropriate evidence, and paired with a predefined escape strategy.
